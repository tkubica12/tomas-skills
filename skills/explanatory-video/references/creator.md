# Creator: explanatory filmmaking

## The contract with the viewer

By default, open with a brief title/orientation scene: name the topic and voice one useful
question or promise of understanding. A few seconds is a production starting
point, not a proven universal optimum. Prefer a transition into the running
example over a separate logo sting. Do not front-load an agenda, prerequisite
catalog or definitions.
Respect an explicit brief that omits it; an excerpt within an already-oriented
lesson does not need another opening. These are authoring defaults, not universal
empirical laws.

Start the first substantive scene with a specific phenomenon worth understanding,
not a historical roll call or promise of a revolutionary insight.
Give enough context for the viewer to care without manufacturing urgency.
Intellectual tension can come from an honest puzzle or a failed prediction.

Write the desired transfer first: "After this, the viewer can predict X when
Y changes, because Z." Specify assumed knowledge and what the film will not
cover. Distinguish prerequisite recall from new inference.

List likely existing beliefs and misconceptions. Draft 3-5 transfer questions
before scripting; their answers should require applying the model, not repeating
a sentence. Each treatment should earn a non-obvious conditional insight:
show what changes a tradeoff and where its direction reverses. Present the
strongest reasonable alternative, not an obviously broken strawman.

Reconstruct one complete chain:

1. A concrete initial situation and a constraint.
2. An operation that changes the situation.
3. The intermediate state and its consequences.
4. A contrast that rules out a tempting but wrong explanation.
5. A boundary where the model stops predicting.

Terminology names roles the viewer has already encountered. Equations earn
their place by reducing ambiguity or enabling a calculation. Introduce units,
variables, conditions, and what is held fixed. Show the bridge at the weakest
"therefore." A deep film is not a longer inventory of named concepts.

At first explanation, show each central term beside the role or representation
it names while the narration names it. Preserve those names in comparisons and
when returning to the concept. A spoken term, an unrelated heading and an
unlabeled diagram do not establish the mapping. Do not turn this into a screen
transcript: short conceptual anchors and optional captions have different jobs.

## Three duration strategies

| Profile | Structural promise | Typical production starting point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| About 3 minutes | One question, one complete mechanism, one contrast, one limit and a transferable takeaway. | Roughly 320-420 Czech or 360-450 English words before timing a voice probe; 5-9 short sequences. |
| 8-12 minutes | One running example developed into a usable model, two or three meaningful failure/contrast cases, one prediction and a small decision framework. | Roughly 1,000-1,500 spoken words; 3-5 chapters; deliberate pause before an answer. |
| 20-35 minutes | A cumulative model with prerequisites introduced just in time, worked derivations, competing designs, failure recovery, structural connections and a diagnostic test. | Roughly 2,600-4,700 spoken words; 6-10 chapters with local closure and a return to the running example. |

Word ranges are planning heuristics, not evidence-based optimal rates or runtime
guarantees. Czech technical narration can be slower. Measure the selected voice.
If a script misses duration, author or edit the argument; do not time-stretch,
repeat material, or add long empty holds to pass a duration check.

The short film must still explain *why*. Remove branches before causal links.
The medium film should be satisfying without the long film. The long film
should justify its extra time through new inferential work, not repetition.
Use chapter boundaries and retrieval pauses in long films; MOOC engagement
observations are not a universal ban on long educational videos.

Close completed reasoning blocks with a short synthesis: what can we now
explain or do, through which mechanism, and what unresolved constraint motivates
the next block? A recap can transform the current diagram into a compact
comparison, retain only its decisive values, or use one explicit bridge sentence.
It must not merely announce the next topic. In a three-minute film, one central
bridge may suffice; medium films need a few, and deep films need local closure
at major argument boundaries. Do not recap every shot, copy the same verbal
formula repeatedly, or reveal the answer to a later prediction.

Budget orientation, comparisons and closure inside the chosen duration. Remove
redundant breadth before causal links; do not bolt on extra scenes and speed up
the speech to compensate.

Keep a separate inference list for each treatment. Compare them before release:
medium must stand alone, and long must add new derivations rather than new
wording. Open the concrete puzzle in the first substantive segment.

## Visual score

Keep a persistent world. A highlighted object should keep its identity and
meaning when a new representation appears. Introduce one relevant change at a
time and retain its consequence long enough to compare.

Choose representations to answer questions:

| Question | Useful representation |
| --- | --- |
| Who can act or knows what? | Participant graph with local knowledge, authority boundary, and directed messages. |
| What happened before a failure? | Event timeline, cursor, durable/volatile layers, and the surviving external state. |
| Why does the quantity change? | Labeled axes, running accumulation, controlled perturbation and units. |
| Are two designs equivalent? | Matched side-by-side traces with the same inputs and failure assumptions. |
| Does a rule generalize? | A near counterexample, prediction pause, then a changed constraint. |
| Which step is necessary? | Build the system incrementally; remove the step and show the failure. |
| What differs between alternatives we just understood? | Progressively revealed comparison with matched criteria and conditional costs/benefits. |
| What have we resolved, and what remains? | Distilled causal chain, retained result or short visual synthesis leading to the next question. |

For a statistics lesson, vary a sample while retaining the population and
show why a denominator matters. For biology, build a feedback pathway and
block one reaction. For history, separate evidence, chronology and competing
interpretations; do not animate an uncertain cause as a proven physical force.

These are options, not required scene types. Biological, social, mathematical
and historical topics need their own honest representations. Never imply
precision, causality or universality that the evidence does not support.

Vary representations when the reasoning task changes, not to satisfy a scene
quota. After diagrams establish mechanisms, a compact comparison table can help
organize differences. Reveal a row or meaningful unit with its spoken claim;
retain column names and semantic identities. Compare the same task and relevant
conditions. State what an advantage costs and when it applies, rather than
assigning unconditional good/bad marks. A short film may need only two rows.

Place labels next to their referents; favor short labels over paragraphs.
Connect dependency edges to their intended objects. An intentional gap should
mean something visible, such as an interrupted message, not an ambiguous endpoint.
Do not read dense on-screen prose over a complex animation. Offer optional
captions separately for accessibility; redundancy advice is not a reason to
exclude deaf viewers or non-native speakers. Do not rely on color alone.

Keep the film frame clean: no persistent source/disclaimer footer, debug shot
ID, progress bar or automatic takeaway strip. Keep provenance, synthetic-voice
disclosure and production metadata in the accompanying materials. Necessary
assumptions belong in the explanation or a normal diagram label at the point
where they matter, not fine print. Author important conclusions as local
closure; do not lose their teaching function when removing screen furniture.

Inspect diagonal and near-horizontal edges, arrowhead joins and moving objects
at actual delivery size and in encoded temporal samples. A shaft must meet the
base of its arrowhead rather than protrude through the tip as a square. Use
antialiased geometry and stable stroke weights; more bitrate cannot fix aliased
source geometry. Compare source and encoded frames before diagnosing compression.

Use easing for object motion, but exact timing for logical state changes.
No perpetual particles suggesting action after a process has completed.
Useful stillness gives the viewer time to reason; decorative motion consumes it.

Reusing a representation is useful; reusing a conclusion after changing its
premise is not. For every reused scene, name the premise delta and the visible
delta that carries it: a failed response must not become consent, a different
key must not reuse the old result, and a changed denominator must change the
comparison. Compare consecutive visual signatures and inspect unexplained
matches. Inventory the longest unchanged spans in mechanism shots; a still
can support reasoning, but it cannot stand in for an unshown transition.

Complete the prediction's premises before the thinking pause and freeze the
informative state during it. Keep answers out of headings, footers and labels
until the reveal. Mark answer surfaces in the score when the backend supports
timing lint. Automated declarations supplement, not replace, semantic review.
Audit geometry as well as text: a completed trace or result-specific boundary
can give away the answer. Do not hide legitimate premises to manufacture
difficulty.

Show the failure that motivates a repair before showing the repaired structure,
unless that failure was just established. Give a tradeoff a visible cost:
which work repeats, which information is lost, or which result can change?
State the conditions of that witness. A possible failure branch is not an
inevitable or observed outcome. Across a cut from competing predictions to a
result, carry the discriminating prediction forward and pair it with the result;
do not turn the comparison into an unstated memory test.

Preserve the kind of information being explained: a recorded decision must
show the selected value, not merely an unrelated completed step. Separate
historical observations from current-world assertions when an outcome is
unknown. In an operation-order comparison, change the visible temporal flow,
not only the outcome labels. When adding a prerequisite, check that its
evidence can exist before the action it gates; do not accidentally create a
cycle through that action's own result.

## Narration and sound

Write for speech, not a paper. Use concrete verbs, varied sentence lengths,
conversational precision and occasional genuine questions. Avoid sales copy,
forced jokes, exaggerated amazement, ritual recaps and unexplained acronyms.
This rejects empty repetition, not meaningful block closure and causal bridges.
Mark a short thinking pause before the answer, not after it.

Keep a pronunciation dictionary. Prefer speaking a technical term naturally
or explaining it in the target language over reading identifiers letter by
letter. Preserve the exact technical spelling in captions or labels.
Do not assume SSML support is identical across speech models.

Probe a representative difficult paragraph. Compare available voices if useful.
Keep speaker identity, rate and style stable across segments. Build timing from
actual audio and regenerate changed narration; stale audio with a new script is
a release blocker. Capture word boundaries when supported; otherwise synthesize
sentence-level segments for honest caption boundaries, or label coarse timing.

Default to no background music for a dense explainer. Music is optional craft,
not a quality requirement. Use only authorized original or properly licensed
assets. Normalize the finished narration and verify peak limits; a useful
starting target is -16 LUFS integrated and no more than -1.5 dBTP, not a universal
platform mandate.

## Preflight and production

Compare a small number of fitting tools against the hardest scene, environment,
font support, licenses, reproducibility and render cost. Avoid a heavyweight
stack just because a famous creator uses it. Avoid a tiny stack when it forces
every explanation into the same three moving boxes.

Render an early scene before writing the whole film into a constrained renderer.
Test actual output resolution and font metrics. Cache speech by text, voice,
style, rate and model; cache video by the score and media content hashes.
Keep a resumable job manifest and do not duplicate paid synthesis on restart.

Use the critic at two levels: argument/score review and rendered-film review.
Tell it which material it can inspect. Every repair must name the artifact
revision and the criterion it is intended to satisfy.
